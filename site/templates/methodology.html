How it works

The rules this atlas follows, so you can judge it for yourself. For plain explanations of specialist words, see the glossary; every source is listed on the sources page.

Claims, not conclusions

The atlas is made of claims: single statements, worded as precisely as the evidence allows. "Herodotus names a people called the Paktyes" and "the Paktyes were ancestors of the Pashtuns" are kept as two separate claims, because the first is about what a text says and the second is a much larger historical argument. Rival accounts are linked to each other and shown side by side.

What kind of evidence?

Every citation is labelled with the kind of evidence it provides. These are types, not a ranking: genetics tells us about ancestry but not language; linguistics tells us about language history but not ancestry.

Verification

A claim marked UNVERIFIED has not yet been checked by a person against its sources. A claim can only become verified once every source it cites has been opened and confirmed. These rules are checked automatically before the site is built.

What kind of group is it?

Popular origin theories often blur different kinds of groups. Each population in the atlas is labelled as one of: a name for a people (ethnonym), an archaeological culture (defined by objects such as pottery and burials; we usually don't know their language), a genetic cluster (defined by DNA), a linguistic group, a polity (state or empire), or a religious community.

What comes next

Version 2 is designed but not built: a PCA view of open ancient-genome data, an etymological database with sound changes, a contributor workflow and a citation graph. See the Version 2 design and the genetics analysis plan.

Maps and dates

Ancient regions and peoples did not have fixed borders. The shapes on the map are rough sketches for orientation, not borders. Dates are approximate unless stated; negative years are BCE.
